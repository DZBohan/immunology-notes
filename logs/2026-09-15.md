## How pattern recognition compresses pathogen diversity

The host encounters thousands of parasites, bacteria, fungi, viruses, and
other infectious threats. Innate immunity does not encode a separate receptor
for every pathogen. Instead, a limited set of pattern-recognition receptors
(PRRs) detects conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) shared
across broad pathogen classes.

The mapping is many-to-many: different pathogens can share a PAMP, and related
receptors on immune or barrier cells can detect overlapping patterns. These
recognition events then converge on a smaller set of effector programmes, such
as inflammation, antimicrobial activity, and antiviral defence.

> **Compression principle:** many pathogens → fewer conserved patterns → a
> limited receptor repertoire → shared effector programmes.

## PAMPs by molecular class

PAMPs are microbial molecular features, not whole pathogens. Major classes and
representative examples are:

| Molecular class | Examples | Typical source |
|-----------------|----------|----------------|
| Nucleic acids | ssRNA, dsRNA, unmethylated CpG DNA | Viruses and bacteria |
| Proteins | Pilin, flagellin | Bacteria |
| Cell-envelope components | LPS, lipoteichoic acid, peptidoglycan | Gram-negative or Gram-positive bacteria |
| Carbohydrates | Mannan, β-glucan, zymosan | Fungi |
| Membrane anchors | GPI anchors | Protozoa |

Representative receptor–ligand pairs include LPS–TLR4/CD14,
lipoprotein/lipoteichoic acid–TLR2, and flagellin–TLR5. Fungal carbohydrates
are recognised by TLRs and C-type lectin receptors. Viral nucleic acids are
usually detected in endosomes or the cytosol rather than at the cell surface.

> **Compartment matters:** a blank under *cell-surface receptor* does not mean
> that the PAMP is invisible. It may instead be detected by an endosomal or
> cytosolic sensor.

## Pattern-recognition molecules: cellular and soluble

Innate pattern recognition is performed by both cell-associated receptors and
soluble molecules. Their location determines which ligands they can encounter
and what response follows.

| System | Location | Representative examples | Major targets |
|--------|----------|-------------------------|---------------|
| TLRs | Plasma membrane or endosome | TLRs 1–9 | Microbial surface molecules and nucleic acids |
| NLRs | Cytosol | NOD1/2; NLRP inflammasomes | Peptidoglycan fragments, crystals, ion fluxes, and lysosomal damage |
| RLRs | Cytosol | RIG-I; MDA5 | Viral RNA |
| Cytosolic DNA sensors | Cytosol | AIM2; STING-associated sensors | Bacterial or viral DNA |
| CLRs | Phagocyte plasma membrane | Mannose receptor; DC-SIGN; Dectin-1/2 | Mannose, fucose, and microbial glucans |
| Scavenger receptors | Phagocyte plasma membrane | CD36 | Microbial lipids and other altered lipid structures |
| Complement | Plasma | Complement proteins | Microbial surfaces |
| Pentraxins | Plasma | C-reactive protein | Selected microbial phospholipids |
| Collectins | Plasma or alveoli | Mannose-binding lectin; surfactant proteins A and D | Microbial carbohydrates and surface structures |
| Ficolins | Plasma | Ficolins | Acetylated sugars and lipoteichoic acid |

Cell-associated receptors initiate signalling inside the sensing cell. Soluble
recognition molecules bind microbes in body fluids, promote opsonisation, and
can activate complement. Complement is therefore part of innate pattern
recognition, but it is not a pattern-recognition receptor family.

> **Location rule:** cell surface → exposed microbial structures; endosome or
> cytosol → internalised or intracellular nucleic acids; body fluids →
> soluble recognition, coating, and complement activation.

### Receptor location and signalling convergence

Different receptor families occupy different compartments and use different
adaptors, yet many converge on a limited set of transcriptional and effector
programmes:

```text
plasma-membrane TLRs ──► MyD88 and/or TRIF ─┐
endosomal TLRs ──────────────────├──► NF-κB and IRFs ──► inflammatory and antiviral genes
cytosolic NOD2 ──► RIPK2 ───────────┘

surface Dectin-1/2 ──► CARD9–BCL10 ──► inflammatory genes, ROS, phagocytosis

soluble MBL, SP-A/SP-D, and pentraxins ──► microbial coating and opsonisation
```

TLR2-containing complexes and TLR4 survey the plasma membrane, whereas TLR9
detects nucleic acid in endosomes. NOD2 detects bacterial peptidoglycan
fragments in the cytosol. Dectin receptors recognise fungal carbohydrates at
the cell surface. The exact adaptor and interferon-regulatory factor depend on
the receptor and cell type; the diagram represents pathway convergence rather
than one identical route for every receptor.

> **Convergence principle:** diverse receptors preserve information about
> location and ligand but reuse a small number of downstream signalling hubs.

## C-type lectin receptors: glycan recognition

C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) recognise carbohydrate and glycolipid patterns
on microbes or damaged host cells. Their expression is cell-type-specific, and
their glycan preferences overlap rather than forming one receptor–one pathogen
pairs.

| Receptor | Major expressing cells | Representative specificity | Signalling role |
|----------|------------------------|----------------------------|-----------------|
| Langerin (CD207) | Langerhans cells | Mannose, fucose, GlcNAc | Glycan capture and internalisation |
| MGL (CD301) | Macrophages and selected dendritic cells | Galactose and GalNAc | Binding, uptake, and immune modulation |
| DC-SIGN (CD209) | Dendritic cells and selected macrophages | Mannose- and fucose-containing glycans | Adhesion, pathogen capture, and signalling modulation |
| Mincle | Activated macrophages and selected B cells | Microbial and host glycolipids | Activating signalling through FcRγ |
| Dectin-2 | Macrophages, dendritic cells, Langerhans cells, and monocytes | α-mannans | Activating signalling through FcRγ |

Mincle and Dectin-2 lack a complete signalling module of their own. They pair
with the FcRγ chain, whose ITAM recruits Syk and activates the CARD9–BCL10
pathway. This promotes inflammatory gene expression and antimicrobial
functions. Other CLRs may specialise more in capture, internalisation, or
modulation than in direct inflammatory activation.

> **CLR principle:** receptor expression + glycan structure + signalling
> adaptor determine the response; no single CLR identifies an entire pathogen.

## CD36 links lipid sensing to innate responses

CD36 is a class B scavenger receptor that binds chemically diverse ligands,
including oxidised LDL, long-chain fatty acids, selected microbial lipids, and
material from damaged or apoptotic cells. It therefore connects nutrient
handling, clearance, and innate danger sensing.

CD36 has short cytoplasmic tails and no intrinsic kinase domain. It signals by
assembling membrane partners such as tetraspanins, integrins, Toll-like
receptors, and the sodium–potassium ATPase. Ligand binding activates Src-family
kinases and downstream modules:

```text
oxidised LDL / lipid ligands ──► CD36 + co-receptors
                                  ├──► NOX ──► ROS
                                  ├──► MAPK ──► NF-κB ──► inflammatory response
                                  ├──► AMPK / PPAR-γ ──► metabolic adaptation
                                  └──► Vav ──► cytoskeletal and migratory responses
```

The resulting effects include lipid uptake and accumulation, migration,
immune activation, differentiation, and cell survival or death. In
macrophages, sustained uptake of oxidised LDL through CD36 promotes foam-cell
formation, linking this innate sensing pathway to atherosclerosis.

Long-chain fatty acids are also normal nutrients; they should not all be
labelled DAMPs. Their inflammatory meaning depends on chemical modification,
concentration, tissue context, and the co-receptors engaged.

> **CD36 principle:** ligand uptake and signalling are coupled, but the
> biological outcome is determined by the ligand and the receptor complex.

## NOD-like receptors: cytosolic sensing

NOD-like receptors (NLRs) are a modular family of cytosolic sensors and immune
regulators. A typical NLR contains three regions:

```text
N-terminal effector domain ── central NACHT domain ── C-terminal LRRs
CARD, PYD, or BIR              ATP-driven oligomerisation     regulation/sensing
```

The N-terminal domain determines which downstream proteins can be recruited.
The NACHT domain enables nucleotide-dependent assembly, while the leucine-rich
repeats help maintain regulation and, in some NLRs, participate in sensing.

| NLR system | Main input | Main output |
|------------|------------|-------------|
| NOD1 | Diaminopimelate-containing peptidoglycan fragments, common in Gram-negative bacteria | RIPK2–NF-κB/MAPK signalling |
| NOD2 | Muramyl dipeptide from Gram-positive and Gram-negative peptidoglycan | RIPK2–NF-κB/MAPK signalling |
| NAIP–NLRC4 | Cytosolic flagellin or bacterial secretion-system proteins | Inflammasome activation |
| NLRP1 | Selected pathogen enzymes or toxins and cellular disruption | Inflammasome activation |
| NLRP3 | Diverse disturbances including potassium efflux, extracellular ATP, crystals, and lysosomal damage | Inflammasome activation |

NOD1 and NOD2 are signalling receptors; they do not normally form canonical
inflammasomes. By contrast, NLRC4, NLRP1, and NLRP3 can assemble inflammasomes,
leading to caspase-1 activation, IL-1β and IL-18 maturation, and pyroptosis.

NAIPs directly recognise flagellin or bacterial secretion-system components
and then activate NLRC4. Likewise, the long list of NLRP3-associated stimuli
does not imply that NLRP3 directly binds every item. It detects shared cellular
consequences of danger, including ion imbalance, organelle damage, and metabolic
stress.

> **NLR principle:** similar molecular architecture does not mean identical
> function; some NLRs signal, some assemble inflammasomes, and some regulate
> transcription or other cellular processes.

## RIG-I-like receptors: cytosolic viral RNA sensing

RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs) detect viral RNA in the cytosol of infected cells.
RIG-I and MDA5 share a central helicase/ATPase region, a C-terminal RNA-binding
domain, and two N-terminal CARDs that transmit the signal. LGP2 has the RNA-
binding regions but lacks CARDs, so it regulates RIG-I and MDA5 rather than
signalling to MAVS directly.

| Sensor | Preferred RNA feature | Signalling capacity |
|--------|-----------------------|---------------------|
| RIG-I | Short base-paired RNA with a blunt end and an exposed 5′-triphosphate or 5′-diphosphate; absent normal cap methylation strengthens recognition | Two CARDs activate MAVS |
| MDA5 | Long double-stranded RNA, often generated during viral replication | Two CARDs activate MAVS |
| LGP2 | Viral RNA; modulates ligand recognition by RIG-I and MDA5 | No CARDs; regulatory rather than directly signalling |

RNA binding and ATP-dependent conformational change expose the CARDs of RIG-I
or MDA5. CARD–CARD interaction then activates MAVS, which is anchored to
mitochondria, peroxisomes, and mitochondria-associated membranes.

```text
viral RNA ──► RIG-I or MDA5 ──► MAVS
                                  ├──► TBK1 / IKKε ──► IRF3 / IRF7
                                  └──► NF-κB
                                               └──► type I interferons + antiviral genes
```

RLRs and endosomal TLR3/7/8 can recognise related viral nucleic acids but in
different compartments: RLRs survey the cytosol, whereas those TLRs survey
endosomes.

> **RLR division of labour:** RIG-I favours short, exposed viral RNA ends;
> MDA5 favours long double-stranded RNA; LGP2 tunes both responses.

## Toll-like receptors: location predicts specificity

TLRs are divided spatially between the plasma membrane and endosomes:

| Location | Receptor | Representative ligand |
|----------|----------|-----------------------|
| Plasma membrane | TLR1/2 | Triacyl bacterial lipoproteins |
| Plasma membrane | TLR2/6 | Diacyl bacterial lipoproteins |
| Plasma membrane | TLR4 | LPS from Gram-negative bacteria |
| Plasma membrane | TLR5 | Bacterial flagellin |
| Endosome | TLR3 | dsRNA |
| Endosome | TLR7/8 | ssRNA |
| Endosome | TLR9 | Unmethylated CpG-rich DNA |

Surface TLRs mainly inspect exposed microbial membrane or protein structures.
Endosomal TLRs inspect nucleic acids delivered into the endocytic pathway. This
spatial separation helps distinguish microbial nucleic acids from host nucleic
acids in their normal compartments.

TLR11, TLR12, and TLR13 shown in the lecture figure are mouse receptors with no
direct functional human equivalents: TLR11/12 recognise protozoan profilin,
whereas TLR13 recognises bacterial ribosomal RNA. Human TLR10 is less well
characterised than the other human TLRs, so individual ligand assignments
should be treated cautiously.

### Historical note

The 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine recognised Bruce Beutler and
Jules Hoffmann for discoveries concerning activation of innate immunity, and
Ralph Steinman for discovering the dendritic cell and its role in adaptive
immunity.

> **TLR location rule:** surface TLRs sample external structures; endosomal
> TLRs sample internalised nucleic acids.

### TLR signalling and the two branches of TLR4

TLR pathways use different adaptors but converge on three major transcriptional
outputs: NF-κB and AP-1 drive pro-inflammatory genes, whereas IRF3 or IRF7
drives type I interferon production.

TLR4 does not bind free LPS by itself. LPS-binding protein first captures LPS
and transfers it to CD14; CD14 then delivers it to the MD-2–TLR4 complex. The
lipid A portion of LPS is the main bioactive structure that activates TLR4.

```text
LPS ──► LBP ──► CD14 ──► MD-2–TLR4
                              │
                              ├── surface: TIRAP–MyD88 ──► NF-κB / AP-1
                              │                          └──► pro-inflammatory cytokines
                              │
                              └── endosome: TRAM–TRIF ──► IRF3 + NF-κB
                                                         └──► type I interferons
```

The common rule is: TLR3 signals through TRIF, TLR4 can signal through both
MyD88 and TRIF, and the other canonical mammalian TLRs signal primarily through
MyD88. Calling the second TLR4 branch **TRIF-dependent** is more informative
than calling it merely "MyD88-independent."

> **Do not memorise every intermediate first:** learn receptor → adaptor →
> transcription factor → output, then add the intermediate kinases later.

#### Intermediate relays in the two TLR4 branches

The MyD88 branch relays the signal through IRAK4/IRAK1, TRAF6, and TAK1. TAK1
then splits the signal: the IKK complex releases NF-κB from IκB-mediated
inhibition, whereas MAP kinases activate AP-1. IRF5 can also support selected
inflammatory genes.

```text
TIRAP–MyD88 ──► IRAK4/1 ──► TRAF6 ──► TAK1
                                         ├──► IKK ──► NF-κB
                                         └──► MAPK ──► AP-1
```

The TRIF branch splits earlier. RIPK1 links TRIF to NF-κB and MAPK/AP-1,
whereas TRAF3 recruits TBK1 and IKKε to activate IRF3 and type I interferon
production.

```text
TRAM–TRIF ──► RIPK1 ──► NF-κB / AP-1 ──► inflammatory genes
          └──► TRAF3 ──► TBK1 / IKKε ──► IRF3 ──► type I interferons
```

Both branches can therefore induce inflammation. The distinguishing output of
the TRIF branch is strong IRF3-dependent type I interferon production.

## DAMPs trigger sterile inflammation

Innate immunity can be activated without an infection. Burns, physical trauma,
and surgery damage cells and release normally intracellular molecules, including
mitochondrial DNA, HMGB1, ATP, mitochondrial formyl peptides, and F-actin.
Outside the cell, these molecules act as damage-associated molecular patterns
(DAMPs).

Macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils detect DAMPs and
produce cytokines, chemokines, reactive oxygen species, neutrophil extracellular
traps, or granule contents. A controlled response clears damaged material and
supports repair. An excessive response causes further tissue injury and releases
more DAMPs, creating a positive-feedback loop that can contribute to systemic
inflammation, organ failure, immune paralysis, and increased susceptibility to
secondary infection.

> **Key distinction:** PAMPs indicate infection; DAMPs indicate tissue damage.
> Both can activate innate pattern-recognition pathways.

### DAMPs operate across spatial scales

Whether a self molecule acts as a DAMP depends on its location, exposure, and
state. ATP is normal inside a cell but signals damage when released; nuclear or
mitochondrial DNA can become inflammatory when it appears in the cytosol or
extracellular space.

| Scale | What the signal means | Representative examples |
|-------|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| Intracellular | A self molecule is misplaced, modified, or associated with cellular stress | Genomic DNA in micronuclei, cytosolic mitochondrial DNA or RNA, ATP, uric acid, amyloid-β, oxidised lipids, and misfolded proteins |
| Intercellular | A damaged cell exposes or releases material that alerts neighbouring cells | HMGB1, ATP, calreticulin, F-actin, S100A8/A9, extracellular RNA, and neutrophil extracellular traps |
| Inter-organ | Tissue-derived molecules enter the circulation and transmit damage signals systemically | Brain-derived HMGB1, liver-derived apolipoprotein C-III, pancreatic F-actin, and haem or haemoglobin species in plasma |

Intercellular DAMPs may bind cell-surface receptors directly or enter a cell and
activate intracellular receptors. Inter-organ signalling provides a route by
which local injury can propagate systemic inflammation.

> **Location principle:** self versus non-self is not enough; innate immunity
> also asks whether a molecule is in the correct compartment and physical state.

### How DAMPs leave cells

DAMP release is not a single process. The same DAMP can leave a cell through
several routes:

1. **Lytic cell death** — necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis
   disrupt the plasma membrane or form pores, releasing intracellular material.
2. **Vesicular or secretory release** — exosomes, ectosomes, lysosomal
   exocytosis, secretory granules, and apoptotic bodies can export selected
   molecules without immediate wholesale membrane rupture.
3. **Extracellular traps** — immune cells expel chromatin and associated
   proteins, placing nuclear material in the extracellular space.

Apoptosis is usually non-lytic and relatively quiet while apoptotic cells are
cleared promptly. It can nevertheless expose or package danger signals, and
failed clearance can progress to secondary necrosis with broader DAMP release.
HMGB1, ATP, histones, heat-shock proteins, extracellular RNA, and cell-free DNA
can therefore each appear through more than one release mechanism.

> **Release-route principle:** immune impact depends not only on which DAMP is
> present, but also on how, where, and in what molecular form it was released.

## Acute inflammation recruits blood-borne defence

Acute inflammation converts local danger detection into vascular and cellular
recruitment:

1. Injury disrupts a physical barrier, allowing microbial entry and causing
   DAMP release.
2. Tissue-resident sentinel cells—especially macrophages, dendritic cells, and
   mast cells—detect PAMPs and DAMPs.
3. Sentinel cells release cytokines, chemokines, histamine, and lipid mediators.
4. Local vessels dilate and become more permeable, allowing fluid and plasma
   proteins to enter the tissue.
5. Complement, antimicrobial proteins, and pre-existing antibodies act on
   microbes in the tissue.
6. Cytokines activate endothelium, adhesion molecules capture circulating
   leukocytes, and chemokines guide them across the vessel wall.
7. Recruited neutrophils and later monocytes phagocytose and kill microbes and
   help clear damaged material.

TNF and IL-1 are major endothelial-activating cytokines, while chemokines
provide directional migration cues. Vasodilation produces redness and heat;
vascular leakage contributes to swelling. These changes are useful locally but
can damage tissue or cause systemic illness when excessive.

Antibodies shown in the diagram are adaptive immune products, not innate
components. However, pre-existing circulating antibodies can enter inflamed
tissue and cooperate with innate effector mechanisms.

> **Purpose of acute inflammation:** contain the threat, deliver soluble and
> cellular effectors, remove the cause, and create conditions for repair.

### TNF-α: one cytokine, context-dependent outcomes

PAMP and DAMP recognition induces TNF-α production. Monocytes and macrophages
are major innate sources; dendritic cells and activated T lymphocytes can also
produce it. TNF functions as a trimer and signals through two receptors:

| Receptor | Key feature | Typical signalling tendency |
|----------|-------------|-----------------------------|
| TNFR1 | Widely expressed; contains an intracellular death domain | TRADD-dependent inflammatory/survival signalling or, in selected contexts, apoptosis or necroptosis |
| TNFR2 | More restricted expression; lacks a death domain | TRAF2-dependent inflammation, survival, proliferation, and tissue regulation |

TNFR1 does not automatically cause apoptosis. Its initial signalling complex
often activates NF-κB and promotes inflammatory gene expression and cell
survival. Cell death occurs when receptor-proximal signalling is reorganised
under the appropriate cellular conditions. TNFR2 more strongly favours
survival and regulatory responses.

Locally, TNF activates endothelium, increases leukocyte recruitment, and helps
contain infection. Excessive systemic TNF can instead drive vascular leakage,
hypotension, organ dysfunction, and shock.

```text
PAMPs / DAMPs ──► producer cell ──► TNF trimer
                                      ├──► TNFR1 ──► inflammation / survival or cell death
                                      └──► TNFR2 ──► inflammation / survival / regulation
```

> **TNF principle:** local, controlled TNF recruits defence; systemic or
> sustained TNF can become a major cause of pathology.

#### How TNFR1 promotes cell survival

The TNFR1 death domain does not make cell death the default outcome. TNF-bound
TNFR1 first assembles membrane-associated **Complex I**, containing TRADD,
RIPK1, TRAF2/5, and cIAP1/2. cIAP proteins add K63-linked ubiquitin chains,
while LUBAC adds M1-linked linear ubiquitin chains. These chains act as
non-degradative signalling scaffolds rather than as labels for protein
destruction.

The ubiquitin scaffold recruits the TAK1–TAB2/3 and IKKα/IKKβ–NEMO complexes.
TAK1 activates JNK and p38 MAP kinases, while IKK activates canonical NF-κB.
NF-κB then induces survival proteins including FLIP and BCL-XL.

```text
TNFR1 ──► Complex I + K63/M1 ubiquitin scaffold
      ├──► TAK1 ──► JNK / p38
      └──► IKK–NEMO ──► NF-κB ──► FLIP + BCL-XL ──► survival
```

TNFR2 also recruits TRAF1/2 and cIAP1/2 and generally favours survival or
regulatory signalling. If the TNFR1 ubiquitin scaffold is lost or reorganised,
RIPK1 can leave Complex I and participate in cytosolic death complexes; this is
how the same receptor can switch from survival to apoptosis or necroptosis.

> **Ubiquitin principle:** K63- and M1-linked chains organise signalling;
> ubiquitin does not always mean proteasomal degradation.

#### TNF as a network amplifier in inflammatory synovium

TNF acts on immune, stromal, and bone-remodelling cells simultaneously. This
network is characteristic of chronic inflammatory synovium, particularly in
rheumatoid arthritis.

| Target | TNF-associated response | Consequence |
|--------|-------------------------|-------------|
| Neutrophils | Recruitment, activation, and prolonged local inflammation | Rapid tissue injury and microbial defence |
| Dendritic cells and T cells | Enhanced antigen-presentation environment and altered Th1, Th17, and Treg responses | Sustained adaptive inflammation or context-dependent regulation |
| Macrophages | TNF, IL-1, IL-12, IL-23, CCL2, and IL-8 production; interaction with type I interferon programmes | Cytokine and chemokine amplification |
| Fibroblast-like synoviocytes | GM-CSF, CXCL10, CCL2, IL-8, VEGF, eicosanoids, and RANKL production | Leukocyte recruitment, angiogenesis, persistent synovitis, and osteoclast support |
| Osteoclast lineage | Direct TNF-receptor effects plus indirect RANKL signalling | Bone resorption and erosion |

TNFR1 generally supports inflammatory and osteoclastogenic effects, whereas
TNFR2 can have regulatory or opposing effects in selected cells. TNF effects on
Treg cells are also context-dependent; the figure should not be read as a
simple instruction that TNF always promotes or suppresses one T-cell lineage.

> **Network principle:** TNF is powerful because it coordinates several cell
> types into a self-reinforcing inflammatory circuit, not because it has only
> one downstream effect.

#### Targeting TNF in inflammatory disease

Anti-TNF agents neutralise TNF before it activates TNFR1 or TNFR2, but their
molecular formats are not interchangeable:

| Agent | Molecular format | Distinguishing feature |
|-------|------------------|------------------------|
| Infliximab | Mouse–human chimeric monoclonal antibody | Binds soluble and membrane TNF; administered intravenously |
| Adalimumab | Fully human monoclonal antibody | Binds soluble and membrane TNF |
| Golimumab | Fully human monoclonal antibody | Binds soluble and membrane TNF |
| Certolizumab pegol | PEGylated humanised Fab′ fragment | Lacks an Fc region and therefore lacks Fc-mediated effector functions |
| Etanercept | Soluble TNFR2 extracellular domain fused to human Fc | Acts as a decoy receptor and binds TNF as well as lymphotoxin-α |

Depending on the agent and regulatory region, anti-TNF therapy is used for
rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis,
and inflammatory bowel disease. The agents are not equally effective in every
disease; notably, etanercept is not an effective treatment for Crohn disease or
ulcerative colitis, whereas several anti-TNF monoclonal antibodies are.

Blocking TNF breaks inflammatory amplification but also removes part of normal
host defence. TNF is important for containing intracellular pathogens and
maintaining granulomas, so anti-TNF treatment increases the risk of serious
infection and reactivation of latent tuberculosis.

The dosing schedules and approved indications shown on a lecture slide are a
time- and jurisdiction-specific snapshot, not a universal prescribing guide.

> **Therapeutic principle:** the same cytokine network that causes chronic
> inflammatory disease also performs essential host-defence functions.

### IL-1: distinct release routes, shared inflammatory signalling

IL-1α and IL-1β bind the same signalling receptor but differ in how they are
produced and released.

**IL-1β** is made mainly by activated monocytes and macrophages as inactive
pro-IL-1β. Its production usually follows two conceptual steps: priming induces
pro-IL-1β expression, and a second danger signal activates an inflammasome.
Caspase-1 then cleaves pro-IL-1β into mature IL-1β. Neutrophil proteases,
including proteinase 3, neutrophil elastase, and cathepsin G, can also process
pro-IL-1β outside the canonical caspase-1 route.

**IL-1α** is often already present inside epithelial and other cells. The
precursor can be biologically active, and necrotic cells release IL-1α as an
alarmin that rapidly reports tissue damage.

```text
IL-1α or IL-1β ──► IL-1R1 + IL-1RAcP ──► MyD88 / IRAK4 ──► NF-κB
                                                        └──► inflammatory genes
```

Two endogenous brakes limit this pathway. IL-1 receptor antagonist competes
for IL-1R1 without activating it, whereas IL-1R2 acts mainly as a decoy receptor
that captures IL-1. Downstream IL-1 responses include neutrophil recruitment,
Th17 and ILC3 responses, prostaglandin E2, RANKL, and matrix
metalloproteinases. These responses support defence but can also drive fever,
cartilage damage, and bone erosion when persistent.

> **IL-1 division of labour:** IL-1β is typically synthesised and proteolytically
> matured after immune activation; IL-1α can act as a preformed alarm released
> by necrotic cells. Both signal through IL-1R1.

#### Local amplification and systemic effects of IL-1

IL-1 amplifies inflammation by acting on several immune-cell populations.
Neutrophils produce prostaglandin E2 and nitric oxide; monocytes produce TNF
and IL-6; Th17, γδ T cells, and ILC3s produce IL-17 and IL-22. IL-1 also
supports osteoclastogenic signals including CSF-1 and RANKL.

These cellular responses connect IL-1 to organ-level outcomes:

- **Cartilage:** matrix-degrading enzymes promote cartilage damage.
- **Bone:** osteoclast activation promotes bone loss.
- **Blood vessels:** intimal inflammation can contribute to atherogenesis.
- **Hypothalamus:** IL-1 contributes to fever and altered pain processing.
- **Pancreas:** sustained IL-1 signalling can contribute to β-cell dysfunction
  or death in inflammatory metabolic disease.

The diagram mainly illustrates persistent or excessive IL-1 activity. During a
controlled acute response, the same pathways support host defence and tissue
repair rather than inevitably causing chronic disease.

### IL-6: classical and trans-signalling

IL-6 uses a two-component receptor system. IL-6 first binds IL-6R; this complex
then recruits and dimerises gp130, activating JAKs and STAT3-dependent gene
transcription.

| Mode | Receptor arrangement | Functional implication |
|------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Classical signalling | IL-6 binds membrane IL-6R on a restricted set of cells, then recruits gp130 | More restricted targeting; includes homeostatic, regenerative, and immune functions |
| Trans-signalling | IL-6 binds soluble IL-6R, and the complex engages gp130 on cells that may lack membrane IL-6R | Broadens the responding cell population and commonly supports inflammation |

Because gp130 is broadly expressed, soluble IL-6R allows IL-6 to act on many
more cells than classical signalling alone. The distinction is useful but not
absolute: biological outcome still depends on cell type, timing, and tissue
context.

Major IL-6-associated outputs include:

- acute-phase protein production by hepatocytes;
- T-cell differentiation and B-cell immunoglobulin production;
- megakaryocyte maturation and platelet production;
- haematopoietic stem-cell and multilineage progenitor support;
- osteoclast activation; and
- proliferation or activation of selected stromal and tumour cells.

> **IL-6 principle:** membrane IL-6R restricts classical signalling; soluble
> IL-6R expands signalling to gp130-positive cells.

### Interferon families and shared antiviral transcription

The three interferon families use distinct receptors but partially overlap in
downstream signalling and gene expression:

| Family | Representative cytokines | Receptor | Main biological emphasis |
|--------|--------------------------|----------|--------------------------|
| Type I | Multiple IFN-α subtypes, IFN-β, IFN-ε, IFN-κ, and IFN-ω | IFNAR1/2 | Broadly distributed antiviral and immunomodulatory response |
| Type II | IFN-γ | IFNGR1/2 | Macrophage activation and cell-mediated immunity |
| Type III | IFN-λ1–4 | IFNLR1 plus IL-10Rβ | Antiviral defence concentrated at epithelial barriers |

Type I interferons bind IFNAR1/2 and activate TYK2 and JAK1. Type III
interferons bind IFNLR1–IL-10Rβ and also activate JAK-family kinases. Both
routes phosphorylate STAT1 and STAT2, which combine with IRF9 to form ISGF3.
ISGF3 binds interferon-stimulated response elements and induces antiviral genes.

```text
type I or III IFN ──► receptor ──► JAK1 / TYK2
                                      └──► STAT1 + STAT2 + IRF9
                                           ──► ISGF3 ──► ISRE ──► ISGs
```

Representative ISG products block different stages of viral infection: OAS
systems promote viral-RNA degradation, IFITM proteins restrict viral entry,
Mx proteins inhibit viral replication, and ISG15 modifies host or viral
proteins. STAT1 homodimers can also bind gamma-activated sequence elements;
this route is especially characteristic of type II interferon signalling.

Type I and III interferons therefore encode similar antiviral instructions,
but receptor distribution changes the scale of the response: type I signalling
is widespread, whereas type III signalling is focused on epithelial surfaces
and tends to limit systemic inflammation.

> **Interferon principle:** cytokine family determines receptor access; shared
> JAK–STAT modules then induce overlapping antiviral genes.

#### Direct antiviral actions of type I interferons

Virus-infected cells release type I interferons that act both on the producing
cell and on neighbouring uninfected cells. IFNAR signalling induces ISGs before
the neighbouring cells are infected, creating an **antiviral state**.

| ISG system | Mechanism | Antiviral result |
|------------|-----------|------------------|
| IFIT proteins | Recognise selected non-self RNA features and interfere with translation machinery | Reduced viral mRNA translation |
| PKR | Activated by dsRNA; phosphorylates eIF2α | Translation arrest and reduced viral protein synthesis |
| OAS–RNase L | dsRNA activates OAS, generating 2′–5′ oligoadenylates that activate RNase L | Viral and cellular RNA degradation |
| Mx GTPases | Assemble into antiviral complexes that interfere with viral replication machinery | Reduced viral gene expression and virion production |
| IFITM proteins | Alter cellular membranes and restrict fusion or entry of selected viruses | Reduced viral entry |

These mechanisms act at different stages—entry, translation, RNA stability,
replication, and assembly—making it harder for a virus to escape through one
single mutation.

#### Type I interferons coordinate innate and adaptive immunity

Type I interferons do more than create a cell-intrinsic antiviral state:

- They increase NK-cell activation and cytotoxicity.
- They promote dendritic-cell migration, maturation, co-stimulatory molecule
  expression, and antigen presentation.
- They can provide a cytokine "signal 3" that supports activated T-cell
  expansion and differentiation.
- Increased MHC class I expression on T cells and other cells can reduce
  inappropriate NK-cell attack.

Antiviral control also changes **antigen load**. Strong ISG activity reduces
viral replication and therefore may reduce the amount of antigen available to
prime adaptive immunity. In a specialised example, virus-permissive
metallophilic macrophages can retain antigen because USP18 restrains interferon
signalling, creating a temporary viral reservoir that supports antigen
presentation. This is context-specific and should not be generalised to every
virus or macrophage population.

> **Coordination principle:** type I interferons balance immediate viral
> restriction with the amount, location, and duration of antigen available to
> activate adaptive immunity.

## Trained immunity and innate immune memory

Innate immune cells can respond differently after a previous challenge. This
phenomenon is called **trained immunity** when the later response is enhanced,
and it is driven by persistent metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming rather
than by somatically rearranged antigen receptors.

| Feature | Adaptive immune memory | Trained innate immunity |
|---------|------------------------|-------------------------|
| Main cells | Antigen-specific T and B lymphocyte clones | Monocytes/macrophages, NK cells, and reprogrammed myeloid progenitors |
| Molecular basis | Clonal selection, expansion, and long-lived memory cells | Altered chromatin accessibility, gene regulation, and cellular metabolism |
| Specificity | High; strongest against the same antigen | Broad; can enhance responses to an unrelated later challenge |
| Later response | Faster and larger antigen-specific response | Increased cytokine production, killing, or other effector functions |
| Duration | Often years to decades | Variable; longer than an acute innate response but generally less durable than adaptive memory |

The two graphs are conceptual rather than directly comparable: the adaptive
panel measures antibody concentration, while the innate panel represents
overall response strength. Their shared message is that prior exposure changes
the magnitude of a later response, but the underlying mechanisms differ.

### Drosophila experiment: heterologous protection

Adult flies received either saline or *Micrococcus luteus* as a training
challenge. Six days later, both groups were challenged with *Staphylococcus
aureus*, and survival was measured for seven days.

```text
saline control  ─┐
                 ├── 6 days ──► S. aureus challenge ──► survival assay
M. luteus train ─┘
```

In two independent experiments, trained flies had higher survival than saline
controls after the second challenge. Because the training and challenge used
different bacterial species, the protection was heterologous rather than
strictly antigen-specific. Drosophila lacks vertebrate-style adaptive immunity,
so the result supports a form of innate immune memory.

The experiment demonstrates a lasting change in host response, but survival
alone does not reveal its mechanism. Distinguishing trained immunity from
residual immune activation requires additional measurements, such as pathogen
burden, baseline inflammation before rechallenge, and persistent metabolic or
epigenetic changes.

> **Memory distinction:** adaptive memory preserves antigen-specific clones;
> trained immunity preserves an altered functional state.

### Central and peripheral trained immunity

The term **trained immunity** was introduced to describe memory-like functional
changes in innate host defence. Training can occur at two connected levels:

| Level | Cells affected | Consequence |
|-------|----------------|-------------|
| Peripheral trained immunity | Mature monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, or NK cells | Epigenetic and metabolic changes enhance later inflammatory or antimicrobial functions |
| Central trained immunity | Haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells in bone marrow | Reprogrammed myelopoiesis repeatedly generates trained monocytes and neutrophils |

Inducers such as β-glucan or BCG vaccination can generate cytokine signals,
including IL-1β, GM-CSF, and interferons, that reach the bone marrow. There,
epigenetic rewiring of haematopoietic stem cells and progenitors can alter the
production or functional state of granulocyte–monocyte descendants.

```text
training stimulus
    ├──► mature innate cells ──► enhanced peripheral response
    └──► bone-marrow HSC ──► MPP ──► GMP ──► trained monocytes/neutrophils
```

Central training helps solve a lifespan problem: circulating monocytes and
neutrophils are relatively short-lived, but reprogrammed progenitors can keep
producing altered descendants after the original stimulus has disappeared.
Epigenetic rewiring changes chromatin accessibility and gene regulation, not
the DNA sequence itself.

This persistence can improve broad antimicrobial protection, but excessive or
maladaptive training may also sustain chronic inflammatory disease.

### Historical origin of the term

Netea and colleagues framed this concept in the 2011 *Cell Host & Microbe*
Perspective, "Trained Immunity: A Memory for Innate Host Defense."

> **Persistence principle:** peripheral training changes existing effector
> cells; central training changes the progenitor supply that continually
> replaces them.

### Candida model: monocyte-dependent trained protection

Quintin and colleagues reported in a 2012 *Cell Host & Microbe* study that a
first *Candida albicans* exposure protected mice during a later challenge
through functional reprogramming of monocytes.

The genetic comparisons identify the responsible immune compartment:

| Mouse model | Interpretation | Training result |
|-------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Wild type | Complete immune system | Prior *Candida* exposure improved survival |
| *Rag1*⁻/⁻ | Lacks mature T and B cells | Protection remained, arguing against classical adaptive memory as the requirement |
| *Ccr2*⁻/⁻ | Impaired mobilisation and recruitment of inflammatory monocytes | Protection was lost, supporting a requirement for the monocyte compartment |

CCR2 deficiency should not be read literally as "no monocytes" in every
tissue; it strongly disrupts classical inflammatory-monocyte trafficking and
therefore tests their contribution to protection.

Mechanistically, fungal β-glucan engages Dectin-1 at a phagocytic synapse. One
branch activates NF-κB and inflammatory cytokines during the acute response.
Phagocytosis and phagolysosomal processing also engage mTOR-dependent metabolic
reprogramming, including changes in glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism.
Together with persistent epigenetic changes, this leaves monocytes with an
enhanced response to later challenge.

```text
fungal β-glucan ──► Dectin-1
                    ├──► NF-κB ──► acute inflammatory cytokines
                    └──► phagocytosis ──► mTOR + metabolic rewiring
                                                  └──► trained response
```

> **Causal logic:** persistence in *Rag1*⁻/⁻ mice shows that T and B cells
> are not required; loss in *Ccr2*⁻/⁻ mice implicates inflammatory
> monocytes.

### NK-cell memory during MCMV infection

Natural killer cells can also generate long-lived, enhanced recall responses.
In C57BL/6 mice infected with murine cytomegalovirus (MCMV), the activating
receptor Ly49H on a subset of NK cells directly recognises the viral m157
protein displayed by infected cells. IL-12 and IL-18 provide additional
inflammatory signals, while activated NK cells produce IFN-γ and cytotoxic
effector responses.

The response proceeds through phases resembling a lymphocyte memory response:

1. **Resting phase:** a small pool of Ly49H-positive NK cells is present before
   infection.
2. **Expansion:** MCMV recognition drives rapid proliferation and effector
   differentiation over several days.
3. **Contraction:** most expanded cells disappear after viral control.
4. **Memory maintenance:** a smaller population of long-lived memory NK cells
   persists for months.
5. **Recall:** reinfection triggers a faster and larger secondary expansion.

```text
MCMV-infected cell displaying m157
              │
              ▼
      Ly49H-positive NK cell + IL-12/IL-18
              │
              ├──► expansion ──► contraction ──► memory NK cell
              └──► IFN-γ and cytotoxic effector activity       │
                                                               ▼
                                                enhanced recall response
```

This is distinct from monocyte trained immunity. The MCMV model involves
direct recognition of a defined viral ligand by an activating NK-cell receptor
and expansion of the responding NK-cell subset. Monocyte training is generally
broader and is maintained mainly through metabolic and epigenetic
reprogramming rather than a clonally expanded antigen-receptor population.

### Neutrophils in trained immunity

Neutrophils are short-lived, but sustained functional changes can arise when
training reprograms granulopoiesis in the bone marrow. The slide presents two
experimental settings.

#### β-glucan training and antitumour activity in mice

Mice received PBS or fungal β-glucan before tumour-cell challenge. β-glucan
reduced subsequent tumour growth in both immunocompetent and *Rag1*⁻/⁻
settings, showing that the effect did not require mature T or B cells.

Adoptive-transfer experiments identified the responsible effector population:
transferring monocytes from trained donors did not reproduce the protection,
whereas transferring neutrophils from β-glucan-trained wild-type donors
reduced tumour growth. Protection was lost when the transferred neutrophils
lacked NCF1, a component required for phagocyte NADPH-oxidase activity. This
supports a neutrophil-mediated, reactive-oxygen-dependent antitumour mechanism.

#### BCG vaccination and neutrophil function

Human neutrophils were sampled before BCG vaccination and again after 14 and
90 days. Their capacity to kill *Candida albicans* was enhanced at both later
time points, as indicated by fewer surviving colony-forming units. NET
formation changed little, and the increase in ROS at day 90 was only a trend
in the displayed analysis (*p* = 0.055), so neither result should be described
as clearly significant from this figure alone. A complementary mouse
experiment showed increased neutrophil ROS production and phagocytosis after
BCG exposure.

```text
β-glucan or BCG
       │
       ▼
trained granulopoiesis in bone marrow
       │
       ▼
functionally reprogrammed neutrophils
       ├──► enhanced microbial killing and phagocytosis
       └──► ROS-dependent antitumour activity in the mouse model
```

Because individual neutrophils do not survive for months, the long-lasting
effect supports **central trained immunity**: altered progenitors continually
generate neutrophils with modified functions.

### Integrated mechanisms of trained immunity

Trained immunity is sustained by three interacting layers rather than by a
change in DNA sequence.

#### 1. Bone-marrow and progenitor reprogramming

Inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IFN-γ, GM-CSF, IL-6, and TNF can alter the
bone-marrow niche, including mesenchymal stromal cells. Long- and short-term
haematopoietic stem cells feed into multipotent and myeloid progenitors, which
then pass through successive granulocyte precursors to produce mature
neutrophils. Reprogramming these early compartments can therefore maintain a
trained phenotype even though mature myeloid cells are short-lived.

```text
LT-HSC/ST-HSC ──► MPP ──► CMP ──► GMP
                                  └──► granulocyte precursors
                                          └──► mature neutrophils
```

#### 2. Metabolic rewiring

Training stimuli such as β-glucan, LPS, or tissue stress alter nutrient and
oxygen sensing. PI3K–AKT–mTOR signalling activates HIF-1α and increases the
glycolytic programme through GLUT1, HK2, PFK, and LDHA. Mitochondrial ROS and
intermediates including succinate, fumarate, acetyl-CoA, and SAM also change.
These molecules are not merely fuels: they regulate chromatin-modifying
enzymes.

#### 3. Epigenetic and RNA reprogramming

Training establishes permissive chromatin marks at inflammatory and
antimicrobial genes. H3K4me3 marks active promoters, while H3K27ac and related
acetylation marks identify active regulatory regions. Acetyl-CoA supplies
histone acetyltransferases, SAM supplies methylation reactions, and fumarate or
succinate can influence demethylases such as KDM5. The slide also shows METTL3
adding m6A to TLR4 mRNA, linking RNA modification to altered TLR4–MyD88–NF-κB
responsiveness.

```text
training stimulus
      ├──► bone-marrow progenitor and niche changes
      ├──► PI3K–AKT–mTOR/HIF-1α ──► metabolic rewiring
      └──► chromatin and RNA modification
                    ▲                       │
                    └── metabolic cofactors ┘
                              │
                              ▼
          stronger inflammatory or antimicrobial response
```

> **Central principle:** metabolism determines the availability of substrates
> and inhibitors for chromatin-modifying enzymes, while chromatin state
> determines which metabolic and immune genes can respond. This
> metabolic–epigenetic crosstalk stabilises the trained phenotype.

## Lecture map

Innate defence operates at three connected levels:

1. **Physical barriers** — skin and mucosa prevent entry.
2. **Innate immune cells** — neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes,
   macrophages, basophils, dendritic cells, mast cells, and natural killer
   cells respond after a barrier is breached. See [[innate-immune-cells]].
3. **Cell-intrinsic innate signalling** — infected cells detect viral material
   and establish an antiviral state.

## Take-home message

1. **PAMPs identify infection.** They are conserved microbial features that
   are absent from, uncommon in, or located differently within healthy host
   cells.
2. **DAMPs identify tissue damage.** Endogenous molecules released, exposed,
   or misplaced during cell stress or injury initiate inflammation and repair.
3. **PRRs detect both forms of danger.** Cell-associated and soluble
   pattern-recognition molecules translate PAMP or DAMP recognition into
   phagocytosis, inflammatory signalling, complement activation, or antiviral
   defence.
4. **Cytokines coordinate the response.** TNF, IL-1, and IL-6 organise
   inflammation and leukocyte recruitment, whereas type I interferons are
   central to limiting viral spread. Their effects are protective when
   controlled but can cause pathology when excessive or persistent.
5. **Innate responses can be trained.** Infection, vaccination, or microbial
   products can reprogramme mature innate cells and bone-marrow progenitors
   through metabolic and epigenetic changes, producing faster or stronger
   later responses.

Unlike classical T- or B-cell memory, trained immunity is generally not based
on somatically rearranged antigen receptors and may enhance responses to an
unrelated second challenge. Some innate lymphocytes, especially NK cells, can
also generate more receptor- and ligand-specific memory-like responses.

```text
PAMPs or DAMPs
      │
      ▼
     PRRs ──► cytokines and effector mechanisms ──► defence and repair
                                                       │
                      metabolic/epigenetic rewiring ◄──┘
                                      │
                                      ▼
                            altered future responses
```
