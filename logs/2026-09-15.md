## How pattern recognition compresses pathogen diversity

The host encounters thousands of parasites, bacteria, fungi, viruses, and
other infectious threats. Innate immunity does not encode a separate receptor
for every pathogen. Instead, a limited set of pattern-recognition receptors
(PRRs) detects conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) shared
across broad pathogen classes.

The mapping is many-to-many: different pathogens can share a PAMP, and related
receptors on immune or barrier cells can detect overlapping patterns. These
recognition events then converge on a smaller set of effector programmes, such
as inflammation, antimicrobial activity, and antiviral defence.

> **Compression principle:** many pathogens → fewer conserved patterns → a
> limited receptor repertoire → shared effector programmes.

## PAMPs by molecular class

PAMPs are microbial molecular features, not whole pathogens. Major classes and
representative examples are:

| Molecular class | Examples | Typical source |
|-----------------|----------|----------------|
| Nucleic acids | ssRNA, dsRNA, unmethylated CpG DNA | Viruses and bacteria |
| Proteins | Pilin, flagellin | Bacteria |
| Cell-envelope components | LPS, lipoteichoic acid, peptidoglycan | Gram-negative or Gram-positive bacteria |
| Carbohydrates | Mannan, β-glucan, zymosan | Fungi |
| Membrane anchors | GPI anchors | Protozoa |

Representative receptor–ligand pairs include LPS–TLR4/CD14,
lipoprotein/lipoteichoic acid–TLR2, and flagellin–TLR5. Fungal carbohydrates
are recognised by TLRs and C-type lectin receptors. Viral nucleic acids are
usually detected in endosomes or the cytosol rather than at the cell surface.

> **Compartment matters:** a blank under *cell-surface receptor* does not mean
> that the PAMP is invisible. It may instead be detected by an endosomal or
> cytosolic sensor.

## Pattern-recognition molecules: cellular and soluble

Innate pattern recognition is performed by both cell-associated receptors and
soluble molecules. Their location determines which ligands they can encounter
and what response follows.

| System | Location | Representative examples | Major targets |
|--------|----------|-------------------------|---------------|
| TLRs | Plasma membrane or endosome | TLRs 1–9 | Microbial surface molecules and nucleic acids |
| NLRs | Cytosol | NOD1/2; NLRP inflammasomes | Peptidoglycan fragments, crystals, ion fluxes, and lysosomal damage |
| RLRs | Cytosol | RIG-I; MDA5 | Viral RNA |
| Cytosolic DNA sensors | Cytosol | AIM2; STING-associated sensors | Bacterial or viral DNA |
| CLRs | Phagocyte plasma membrane | Mannose receptor; DC-SIGN; Dectin-1/2 | Mannose, fucose, and microbial glucans |
| Scavenger receptors | Phagocyte plasma membrane | CD36 | Microbial lipids and other altered lipid structures |
| Complement | Plasma | Complement proteins | Microbial surfaces |
| Pentraxins | Plasma | C-reactive protein | Selected microbial phospholipids |
| Collectins | Plasma or alveoli | Mannose-binding lectin; surfactant proteins A and D | Microbial carbohydrates and surface structures |
| Ficolins | Plasma | Ficolins | Acetylated sugars and lipoteichoic acid |

Cell-associated receptors initiate signalling inside the sensing cell. Soluble
recognition molecules bind microbes in body fluids, promote opsonisation, and
can activate complement. Complement is therefore part of innate pattern
recognition, but it is not a pattern-recognition receptor family.

> **Location rule:** cell surface → exposed microbial structures; endosome or
> cytosol → internalised or intracellular nucleic acids; body fluids →
> soluble recognition, coating, and complement activation.

### Receptor location and signalling convergence

Different receptor families occupy different compartments and use different
adaptors, yet many converge on a limited set of transcriptional and effector
programmes:

```text
plasma-membrane TLRs ──► MyD88 and/or TRIF ─┐
endosomal TLRs ──────────────────├──► NF-κB and IRFs ──► inflammatory and antiviral genes
cytosolic NOD2 ──► RIPK2 ───────────┘

surface Dectin-1/2 ──► CARD9–BCL10 ──► inflammatory genes, ROS, phagocytosis

soluble MBL, SP-A/SP-D, and pentraxins ──► microbial coating and opsonisation
```

TLR2-containing complexes and TLR4 survey the plasma membrane, whereas TLR9
detects nucleic acid in endosomes. NOD2 detects bacterial peptidoglycan
fragments in the cytosol. Dectin receptors recognise fungal carbohydrates at
the cell surface. The exact adaptor and interferon-regulatory factor depend on
the receptor and cell type; the diagram represents pathway convergence rather
than one identical route for every receptor.

> **Convergence principle:** diverse receptors preserve information about
> location and ligand but reuse a small number of downstream signalling hubs.

## Toll-like receptors: location predicts specificity

TLRs are divided spatially between the plasma membrane and endosomes:

| Location | Receptor | Representative ligand |
|----------|----------|-----------------------|
| Plasma membrane | TLR1/2 | Triacyl bacterial lipoproteins |
| Plasma membrane | TLR2/6 | Diacyl bacterial lipoproteins |
| Plasma membrane | TLR4 | LPS from Gram-negative bacteria |
| Plasma membrane | TLR5 | Bacterial flagellin |
| Endosome | TLR3 | dsRNA |
| Endosome | TLR7/8 | ssRNA |
| Endosome | TLR9 | Unmethylated CpG-rich DNA |

Surface TLRs mainly inspect exposed microbial membrane or protein structures.
Endosomal TLRs inspect nucleic acids delivered into the endocytic pathway. This
spatial separation helps distinguish microbial nucleic acids from host nucleic
acids in their normal compartments.

TLR11, TLR12, and TLR13 shown in the lecture figure are mouse receptors with no
direct functional human equivalents: TLR11/12 recognise protozoan profilin,
whereas TLR13 recognises bacterial ribosomal RNA. Human TLR10 is less well
characterised than the other human TLRs, so individual ligand assignments
should be treated cautiously.

### Historical note

The 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine recognised Bruce Beutler and
Jules Hoffmann for discoveries concerning activation of innate immunity, and
Ralph Steinman for discovering the dendritic cell and its role in adaptive
immunity.

> **TLR location rule:** surface TLRs sample external structures; endosomal
> TLRs sample internalised nucleic acids.

### TLR signalling and the two branches of TLR4

TLR pathways use different adaptors but converge on three major transcriptional
outputs: NF-κB and AP-1 drive pro-inflammatory genes, whereas IRF3 or IRF7
drives type I interferon production.

TLR4 does not bind free LPS by itself. LPS-binding protein first captures LPS
and transfers it to CD14; CD14 then delivers it to the MD-2–TLR4 complex. The
lipid A portion of LPS is the main bioactive structure that activates TLR4.

```text
LPS ──► LBP ──► CD14 ──► MD-2–TLR4
                              │
                              ├── surface: TIRAP–MyD88 ──► NF-κB / AP-1
                              │                          └──► pro-inflammatory cytokines
                              │
                              └── endosome: TRAM–TRIF ──► IRF3 + NF-κB
                                                         └──► type I interferons
```

The common rule is: TLR3 signals through TRIF, TLR4 can signal through both
MyD88 and TRIF, and the other canonical mammalian TLRs signal primarily through
MyD88. Calling the second TLR4 branch **TRIF-dependent** is more informative
than calling it merely "MyD88-independent."

> **Do not memorise every intermediate first:** learn receptor → adaptor →
> transcription factor → output, then add the intermediate kinases later.

## DAMPs trigger sterile inflammation

Innate immunity can be activated without an infection. Burns, physical trauma,
and surgery damage cells and release normally intracellular molecules, including
mitochondrial DNA, HMGB1, ATP, mitochondrial formyl peptides, and F-actin.
Outside the cell, these molecules act as damage-associated molecular patterns
(DAMPs).

Macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils detect DAMPs and
produce cytokines, chemokines, reactive oxygen species, neutrophil extracellular
traps, or granule contents. A controlled response clears damaged material and
supports repair. An excessive response causes further tissue injury and releases
more DAMPs, creating a positive-feedback loop that can contribute to systemic
inflammation, organ failure, immune paralysis, and increased susceptibility to
secondary infection.

> **Key distinction:** PAMPs indicate infection; DAMPs indicate tissue damage.
> Both can activate innate pattern-recognition pathways.

### DAMPs operate across spatial scales

Whether a self molecule acts as a DAMP depends on its location, exposure, and
state. ATP is normal inside a cell but signals damage when released; nuclear or
mitochondrial DNA can become inflammatory when it appears in the cytosol or
extracellular space.

| Scale | What the signal means | Representative examples |
|-------|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| Intracellular | A self molecule is misplaced, modified, or associated with cellular stress | Genomic DNA in micronuclei, cytosolic mitochondrial DNA or RNA, ATP, uric acid, amyloid-β, oxidised lipids, and misfolded proteins |
| Intercellular | A damaged cell exposes or releases material that alerts neighbouring cells | HMGB1, ATP, calreticulin, F-actin, S100A8/A9, extracellular RNA, and neutrophil extracellular traps |
| Inter-organ | Tissue-derived molecules enter the circulation and transmit damage signals systemically | Brain-derived HMGB1, liver-derived apolipoprotein C-III, pancreatic F-actin, and haem or haemoglobin species in plasma |

Intercellular DAMPs may bind cell-surface receptors directly or enter a cell and
activate intracellular receptors. Inter-organ signalling provides a route by
which local injury can propagate systemic inflammation.

> **Location principle:** self versus non-self is not enough; innate immunity
> also asks whether a molecule is in the correct compartment and physical state.

### How DAMPs leave cells

DAMP release is not a single process. The same DAMP can leave a cell through
several routes:

1. **Lytic cell death** — necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis
   disrupt the plasma membrane or form pores, releasing intracellular material.
2. **Vesicular or secretory release** — exosomes, ectosomes, lysosomal
   exocytosis, secretory granules, and apoptotic bodies can export selected
   molecules without immediate wholesale membrane rupture.
3. **Extracellular traps** — immune cells expel chromatin and associated
   proteins, placing nuclear material in the extracellular space.

Apoptosis is usually non-lytic and relatively quiet while apoptotic cells are
cleared promptly. It can nevertheless expose or package danger signals, and
failed clearance can progress to secondary necrosis with broader DAMP release.
HMGB1, ATP, histones, heat-shock proteins, extracellular RNA, and cell-free DNA
can therefore each appear through more than one release mechanism.

> **Release-route principle:** immune impact depends not only on which DAMP is
> present, but also on how, where, and in what molecular form it was released.

## Lecture map

Innate defence operates at three connected levels:

1. **Physical barriers** — skin and mucosa prevent entry.
2. **Innate immune cells** — neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes,
   macrophages, basophils, dendritic cells, mast cells, and natural killer
   cells respond after a barrier is breached. See [[innate-immune-cells]].
3. **Cell-intrinsic innate signalling** — infected cells detect viral material
   and establish an antiviral state.

## RNA-virus sensing shown in the lecture summary

```text
cytosolic viral RNA
    ├── RIG-I ──┐
    └── MDA5  ──┴──► MAVS ──┬──► IRF3 ──► interferons
                            └──► NF-κB ──► inflammatory cytokines

endosomal viral RNA
    └── TLR3 ──► TRIF ──────┬──► IRF3
                            └──► NF-κB

interferon ──► IFN receptor ──► JAK ──► STAT ──► ISGs ──► antiviral state
```

## Take-home message

Innate immunity is not only a collection of immune cells. Individual infected
cells also sense viral RNA: RIG-I and MDA5 signal through MAVS, whereas TLR3
signals through TRIF. Both routes activate IRF3 and NF-κB. The resulting
interferons then signal through JAK–STAT to induce interferon-stimulated genes
in the infected cell and neighbouring cells.
